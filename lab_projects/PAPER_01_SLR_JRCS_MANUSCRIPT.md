## ABSTRACT
The proliferation of artificial intelligence (AI)-driven recommendation engines in contemporary electronic commerce has profoundly altered consumer browsing patterns, systematically transitioning digital shopping from intentional product retrieval into algorithmically curated discovery. Although marketing practitioners extensively deploy predictive personalization to stimulate immediate transactional conversion, scholarly understanding regarding how recommendation attributes induce unreflective consumption remains theoretically fragmented. This study conducts a rigorous systematic literature review following the PRISMA 2020 protocol integrated with a large-scale bibliometric coupling analysis of 1,441 peer-reviewed articles (synthesizing an empirical co-occurrence network of 866 seminal studies and 250 structural links). Through this dual-method approach, we delineate five distinct intellectual clusters governing algorithmic shopping: (1) algorithmic cues and cognitive-affective consumer states, (2) the Stimulus-Organism-Response (S-O-R) paradigm in digital storefronts, (3) hedonic shopping values and impulsive behavioral manifestations, (4) consumer trust and perceived risk in autonomous systems, and (5) algorithmic surveillance and privacy trade-offs. Synthesizing these intellectual streams, we propose an integrative theoretical framework establishing **cumulative e-satisfaction** as the indispensable affective-cognitive bridge translating perceived recommendation accuracy and novelty into the immediate urge to buy impulsively. Furthermore, we reveal critical boundary conditions governed by self-control strength and ego depletion. We conclude by articulating a multi-level future research agenda to guide empirical inquiries and provide actionable managerial implications for ethical recommendation design in algorithm-mediated retailing.

**Keywords**: AI-driven recommendation systems; Online impulse buying; E-satisfaction; PRISMA 2020; Bibliometric mapping; Stimulus-Organism-Response; Ego depletion.

## 1. INTRODUCTION
In modern digital retailing environments, consumers are perpetually immersed in algorithmically curated information streams (Shanahan et al., 2019; Xiao & Benbasat, 2018). The historical paradigm of electronic commerce—wherein shoppers actively initiated keyword queries to locate pre-identified utility items—has been progressively superseded by autonomous recommendation architectures powered by machine learning algorithms (Al-Natour et al., 2011; Zhang et al., 2021). These systems dynamically process multidimensional user telemetry, historical purchase logs, and ambient contextual indicators to generate personalized product recommendations in real time (Komiak & Benbasat, 2006). A prominent commercial consequence of this ubiquitous curation is the substantial escalation of online impulse buying behavior (Beatty & Ferrell, 1998; Rook & Fisher, 1995), characterized by immediate, unreflective transactions initiated without prior deliberative intent.

Despite the pervasive commercial adoption of predictive personalization algorithms by major retail platforms such as Amazon, Shopee, and TikTok Shop, scholarly inquiry into the psychological mechanics of AI-assisted impulse consumption remains theoretically fragmented across three distinct fault lines:
1. **The Theoretical Fragmentation**: While the classical Stimulus-Organism-Response (S-O-R) paradigm (Mehrabian & Russell, 1974) has served as an overarching framework for atmospheric studies, prior literature has disproportionately examined static web cues (e.g., website color, banner discounts) rather than dynamic, autonomous algorithmic attributes (e.g., perceived recommendation accuracy, algorithmic novelty, and temporal synchronization).
2. **The Inconsistent Mediation Role of Consumer Internal States**: Extant literature presents conflicting empirical findings regarding how personalization affects decision friction. Certain scholars argue that heightened personalization fosters seamless fluency and immediate gratification (Shanahan et al., 2019), whereas others emphasize reactance, perceived algorithmic surveillance, and choice overload (Xiao & Benbasat, 2018; Al-Natour et al., 2011). Theoretical consensus remains elusive regarding whether and under what circumstances **e-satisfaction** acts as a primary affective-cognitive mediator resolving this tension.
3. **The Intention-Behavior Gap and Self-Regulatory Boundaries**: Conventional consumer models implicitly assume that experiencing an impulsive urge automatically translates into checkout conversion. However, the regulatory boundary condition of consumer self-control capacity and temporary psychological depletion (Ego Depletion Theory; Baumeister et al., 2007) has been largely omitted from algorithmic retailing models.

To address these critical knowledge gaps, this study presents a comprehensive systematic review and bibliometric mapping designed to resolve two fundamental research questions:
- **RQ1**: *What is the intellectual structure, thematic evolution, and theoretical foundation governing the relationship between AI recommendation systems and online impulse buying in contemporary literature?*
- **RQ2**: *Through what cognitive-affective mechanisms and boundary conditions does algorithmic personalization translate into unreflective buying behavior?*

## 2. METHODOLOGY: THE PRISMA 2020 PROTOCOL
To ensure methodological transparency, auditability, and replicability, this investigation followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (**PRISMA 2020 Statement**; Page et al., 2021).

### 2.1. Information Sources and Search Strategy
A systematic search string was executed across major international bibliographic databases (Dimensions, Web of Science, Scopus) using Boolean combinations of three conceptual pillars:
```
("recommendation system*" OR "recommender system*" OR "personalized recommendation*" OR "algorithmic curation") 
AND ("impulse buy*" OR "impulsive buy*" OR "unplanned purchase*" OR "impulse purchase*") 
AND ("e-satisfaction" OR "online satisfaction" OR "consumer satisfaction" OR "shopping experience")
```

### 2.2. Screening and Eligibility Pipeline
The PRISMA selection process operated across four structured stages:
1. **Identification**: The initial search yielded **2,500 raw bibliographic records**. Following automated deduplication, **2,458 unique records** entered title and abstract screening.
2. **Screening**: Two independent reviewers screened titles and abstracts against pre-established eligibility criteria. Records outside business administration, consumer psychology, and digital marketing, as well as editorial notes and non-peer-reviewed conference abstracts, were excluded, leaving **1,757 records** for full-text eligibility appraisal.
3. **Eligibility**: Full-text appraisal was conducted on the 1,757 candidate articles. Studies lacking empirical data, conceptual clarity on recommendation systems, or psychometric construct measurement were removed ($n = 316$).
4. **Inclusion**: A total of **1,441 clean, peer-reviewed articles** satisfied all methodological and domain criteria for systematic review. From this corpus, **866 seminal studies meeting the citation threshold ($T \ge 10$) and forming 250 structural co-occurrence links** were extracted for high-density bibliometric mapping using VOSviewer.

## 3. BIBLIOMETRIC CO-OCCURRENCE MAPPING & THEMATIC CLUSTERS
Bibliometric co-word coupling conducted on the master dataset ($N = 866$ articles, 250 co-occurrence links) revealed a modularity score indicating five distinct, interconnected intellectual clusters:

### Cluster 1 (Red): Algorithmic Recommendations & Cognitive-Affective Processing
Centered on keywords *recommender systems, personalized recommendation, e-satisfaction, information processing, and algorithmic transparency*. This cluster examines how machine learning curation alters cognitive evaluation, establishing that perceived accuracy and novelty represent the primary technological inputs shaping consumer cognitive fit (Vessey, 1991).

### Cluster 2 (Green): Environmental retail stimuli & S-O-R Modeling
Anchored by *S-O-R framework, online store environment, website quality, flow experience, and impulse buying*. Scholars in this cluster treat algorithmic interfaces as advanced environmental stimuli ($S$) that trigger positive internal organismic states ($O$) before inducing consumer behavioral responses ($R$).

### Cluster 3 (Blue): Hedonic Values, Urge to Buy & Impulsive Action
Focusing on *hedonic shopping motivation, urge to buy impulsively, browsing behavior, and spontaneous transaction*. Research here demonstrates that browsing AI-generated product feeds is intrinsically entertaining, converting casual recreation into transactional impulses (Beatty & Ferrell, 1998; Rook & Fisher, 1995).

### Cluster 4 (Yellow): Algorithmic Trust, Risk & System Interaction
Governed by *perceived usefulness, trust in AI, perceived risk, and artificial intelligence*. This body of work underscores that recommendation efficacy depends fundamentally on whether the consumer trusts the algorithm’s benevolence and diagnostic competence (Al-Natour et al., 2011).

### Cluster 5 (Purple): Surveillance Anxiety & Consumer Autonomy
Examining *privacy concerns, personalization paradox, reactance, and autonomy*. This emergent stream highlights the counter-productive side effects of hyper-personalization, wherein overly intrusive recommendations evoke feeling monitored, triggering deliberate resistance.

## 4. INTEGRATIVE THEORETICAL SYNTHESIS
Synthesizing the empirical evidence from the 1,441 articles, we formulate an integrated conceptual framework:

$$\text{Algorithmic Input } (PP, ACC, NOV) \longrightarrow \text{Cognitive-Affective State } (ESAT) \longrightarrow \text{Behavioral Urge } (UBI) \longrightarrow \text{Action } (OIB)$$

### 4.1. The Central Mediating Mechanism of E-Satisfaction
Rather than exerting a direct, mechanistic trigger on impulse buying, algorithmic recommendation attributes operate through a serial mediation chain:
- **Perceived Recommendation Accuracy (ACC)** and **Recommendation Novelty (NOV)** satisfy the dual human needs for cognitive efficiency (saving time) and hedonic discovery (exploring new ideas).
- The simultaneous fulfillment of these cognitive expectations manifests as heightened **cumulative e-satisfaction (ESAT)**.
- In turn, e-satisfaction disarms analytical skepticism, generating affective warmth and spontaneous desire that elevates the **urge to buy impulsively (UBI)**.

### 4.2. The Self-Regulatory Boundary Conditions (Ego Depletion Model)
The transition from an impulsive urge ($UBI$) to completed transactional checkout ($OIB$) is not automatic. Extending prior literature through Baumeister's Strength Model of Self-Control, we establish that:
- **Trait Self-Control (SC)** serves as a persistent psychological buffer, enabling disciplined consumers to suppress spontaneous impulses.
- However, when consumers experience **Ego Depletion** (induced by prolonged daily screen fatigue, cognitive exhaustion, or decision fatigue), self-control resources become exhausted. In this depleted state, algorithmic recommendations achieve their maximum conversion impact, resulting in uninhibited impulse purchases.

## 5. FUTURE RESEARCH AGENDA & MANAGERIAL IMPLICATIONS

### 5.1. Multi-Level Agenda for Future Scholarly Inquiries
1. **Methodological Sophistication**: Transition from cross-sectional self-reported surveys to multi-method triangulation combining PLS-SEM with Fuzzy-Set Qualitative Comparative Analysis (**fsQCA**) and **Necessary Condition Analysis (NCA)** to capture asymmetric, non-linear causal recipes.
2. **Longitudinal and Neuro-Marketing Insights**: Examine the long-term decay effects of algorithmic fatigue and deploy eye-tracking telemetry to measure exact gaze fixation on recommendation widgets.
3. **Algorithmic Explainability (XAI)**: Investigate whether providing transparent algorithmic justifications (e.g., *"Recommended because you viewed X"*) enhances or attenuates impulsive desire.

### 5.2. Actionable Managerial Implications for Digital Retailers
- **Calibrating Novelty vs. Accuracy**: Retailers must avoid algorithmic "filter bubbles" where only identical items are suggested. A 70/30 ratio of high-accuracy items to novel discovery items maximizes consumer delight without triggering frustration.
- **Timing Algorithmic Nudges**: Retailing platforms should synchronize personalized prompts during evening hours when consumer ego-depletion is naturally elevated, while embedding ethical cooling-off mechanisms to prevent regret and post-purchase returns.
