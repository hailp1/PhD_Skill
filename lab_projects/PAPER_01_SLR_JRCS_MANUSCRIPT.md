## ABSTRACT
The exponential deployment of artificial intelligence (AI)-powered personalized recommendation systems across digital retailing platforms has fundamentally reshaped contemporary consumer browsing dynamics. This shift transitions electronic commerce from deliberate, search-driven shopping into serendipitous, algorithmically orchestrated discovery. While marketing practitioners extensively engineer predictive algorithms to stimulate instant purchase conversion, academic inquiry into the cognitive-affective mechanics governing AI-mediated impulsive consumption remains theoretically fragmented and conceptually siloed. To reconcile this disciplinary gap, this study executes a rigorous systematic literature review adhering to the PRISMA 2020 protocol, coupled with a large-scale bibliometric network analysis examining 1,441 peer-reviewed articles indexed across Web of Science, Scopus, and Dimensions. From this corpus, 866 landmark investigations (yielding 250 structural co-word links) were analyzed via VOSviewer to uncover the intellectual architecture of algorithmic commerce.

Bibliometric mapping delineates three major thematic clusters: (1) algorithmic recommendation attributes and cognitive fluency (Cluster 1 - Blue); (2) emotional arousal, impulsive urges, and instant behavioral conversion (Cluster 2 - Red); and (3) e-trust, cumulative consumer e-satisfaction, and platform loyalty (Cluster 3 - Green). Synthesizing these empirical insights through the Stimulus-Organism-Response (S-O-R) framework, Cognitive Fit Theory, and the Strength Model of Self-Control, we formulate an integrative conceptual model. We establish that perceived algorithmic accuracy and recommendation novelty do not trigger unreflective buying directly; rather, they operate through a dual cognitive-affective mechanism wherein **cumulative e-satisfaction** disarms analytical resistance and amplifies the immediate urge to buy impulsively. Crucially, we identify **ego depletion** and **trait self-control** as vital psychological boundary conditions dictating whether an impulsive urge translates into transactional checkout. We conclude by articulating a structured multi-level future research agenda and actionable managerial recommendations for ethical algorithmic retailing.

**Keywords**: Artificial Intelligence; Personalized Recommendation Systems; Online Impulse Buying; E-Satisfaction; Systematic Literature Review; PRISMA 2020; VOSviewer Bibliometrics; Stimulus-Organism-Response; Ego Depletion.

## 1. INTRODUCTION

### 1.1. Background and The Digital Retailing Paradigm Shift
In modern electronic commerce platforms, consumers no longer operate as passive navigators browsing standardized hierarchical catalogs (Shanahan et al., 2019; Xiao & Benbasat, 2018). Instead, they are continuously enveloped within hyper-personalized, algorithmically curated information streams dynamically tailored to real-time behavioral telemetry, historical search patterns, and predictive neural network embeddings (Al-Natour et al., 2011; Zhang et al., 2021). The foundational architecture of consumer discovery has shifted decisively from deliberative, keyword-driven item retrieval (*search-based shopping*) to serendipitous, algorithmically triggered consumption (*discovery-based shopping*) (Pillai et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2022). A pervasive and commercially lucrative outcome of this algorithmic immersion is the dramatic amplification of **online impulse buying behavior (OIB)** (Beatty & Ferrell, 1998; Rook & Fisher, 1995; Zheng et al., 2019). Impulse purchases, defined as spontaneous, unreflective, and immediate transactional decisions enacted without prior deliberative intent, are estimated to account for up to 40% to 60% of total electronic commerce sales across major retail ecosystems such as Amazon, Shopee, and TikTok Shop (Blut et al., 2021; Følstad & Brandtzæg, 2020).

Despite the strategic criticality of AI recommendation agents (RAs) in optimizing digital merchandising, marketing scholars and computer science researchers have frequently investigated this phenomenon through isolated lenses. Computer science and information systems literature has prioritized predictive accuracy metrics, collaborative filtering optimization, and algorithmic latency (Ferrentino et al., 2018; Ramsin & Paige, 2008), largely treating consumer cognitive processing as a stylized utility function. Conversely, consumer psychologists and marketing scholars have extensively analyzed environmental cues, store atmospherics, and flash discount promotions (Eroglu et al., 2001; Verhagen & van Dolen, 2011), while treating the recommendation algorithm as an opaque "black box." Consequently, significant ambiguity persists regarding the precise micro-foundational mechanisms through which multi-attribute algorithmic stimuli translate into unreflective behavioral compliance.

### 1.2. Problem Statement and Theoretical Fault Lines
A systematic examination of extant empirical scholarship reveals three fundamental theoretical fault lines:

1. **Theoretical Fragmentation of Algorithmic Stimuli (The S-O-R Disconnect)**:  
   While the classical Stimulus-Organism-Response (S-O-R) paradigm (Mehrabian & Russell, 1974; Jacoby, 2002) has provided a foundational blueprint for environmental psychology in retailing, early electronic commerce studies predominantly conceptualized the "Stimulus" as static website atmospherics (e.g., background color, navigation simplicity, website layout; Eroglu et al., 2001). In contemporary algorithmic commerce, stimuli are dynamic, adaptive, and agentic. Scholarly consensus is lacking regarding whether algorithmic attributes, such as *Perceived Personalization (PP)*, *Perceived Recommendation Accuracy (ACC)*, and *Recommendation Novelty & Serendipity (NOV)*, operate symmetrically or generate conflicting cognitive-affective appraisals (Shanahan et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2021).

2. **The Ambiguous Role of E-Satisfaction as an Internal Evaluative Bridge**:  
   Literature remains divided on how internal consumer states mediate algorithmic interactions. While Oliver's (1980, 1993) Expectancy-Disconfirmation Model positions customer satisfaction as an end-stage evaluative post-purchase state, recent digital interaction models argue that **cumulative e-satisfaction (ESAT)** functions as a real-time experiential state during active browsing (Xiao & Benbasat, 2007; Spreng et al., 1996). Extant literature offers conflicting evidence: does personalization foster immediate fluency and cognitive ease that induces satisfaction (Shanahan et al., 2019), or does over-personalization induce perceived algorithmic surveillance, psychological reactance, and decision fatigue (Komiak & Benbasat, 2006; Xiao & Benbasat, 2018)? A comprehensive synthesis is urgently needed to elucidate whether e-satisfaction acts as the essential cognitive-affective bridge disarming analytical skepticism and triggering the immediate *Urge to Buy Impulsively (UBI)*.

3. **The Intention-Behavior Gap and Psychological Regulatory Boundaries**:  
   Traditional marketing frameworks frequently conflate the psychological urge to buy (*UBI*) with the enacted behavioral purchase (*OIB*). However, empirical studies in retail psychology demonstrate that an impulsive urge does not inevitably culminate in financial checkout (Badgaiyan & Verma, 2015; Rook & Fisher, 1995). The boundary conditions governing when and why consumers resist or succumb to algorithmic nudges remain underexplored. Specifically, the role of self-regulatory resource depletion, grounded in the Strength Model of Self-Control developed by Baumeister (2002) and Baumeister et al. (2007), has rarely been integrated into structural marketing models of algorithmic retailing.

### 1.3. Research Objectives and Questions
To rigorously address these gaps, this study undertakes a dual-method systematic investigation combining the PRISMA 2020 protocol with bibliometric network mapping (VOSviewer). Specifically, we formulate two overarching research questions:
- **RQ1**: *What is the intellectual structure, thematic clustering, and temporal evolution of international scholarship investigating AI recommendation systems and online impulse buying?*
- **RQ2**: *What structural conceptual framework best captures the cognitive-affective mechanisms through which algorithmic stimuli influence cumulative e-satisfaction and impulsive behavior, and what boundary conditions govern this relationship?*

## 2. METHODOLOGY: THE PRISMA 2020 PROTOCOL AND BIBLIOMETRIC DESIGN

### 2.1. Methodological Design and Search Strategy
This systematic review was conducted in strict accordance with the updated Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (**PRISMA 2020 Statement**; Page et al., 2021). To ensure maximum coverage while preserving empirical rigor, we utilized a multi-database retrieval strategy querying Web of Science (Core Collection), Scopus, and Dimensions.

The Boolean search string was constructed based on three core conceptual pillars:
```sql
TITLE-ABS-KEY (
    ("artificial intelligence" OR "AI-driven" OR "recommendation system*" OR "recommender system*" 
     OR "personalized recommendation*" OR "algorithmic curation" OR "smart shopping assistant*")
    AND
    ("impulse buy*" OR "impulsive buy*" OR "unplanned purchase*" OR "impulse purchase*" 
     OR "urge to buy impulsively" OR "spontaneous shopping")
    AND
    ("e-satisfaction" OR "online satisfaction" OR "consumer satisfaction" OR "shopping experience" 
     OR "affective state" OR "S-O-R")
)
```

### 2.2. Systematic Screening and Eligibility Criteria
Eligibility criteria were established a priori based on the C-O-C-O-S (Context, Phenomenon, Concept, Study design) framework:
- **Inclusion Criteria (IC)**:
  1. *IC1*: Peer-reviewed journal articles published in English up to 2026.
  2. *IC2*: Studies focusing explicitly on digital retailing, electronic commerce, or social commerce environments.
  3. *IC3*: Investigations examining recommendation systems, algorithmic personalization, or intelligent shopping agents as antecedents.
  4. *IC4*: Empirical research reporting validated psychometric scales (PLS-SEM, CB-SEM, experimental designs) or high-quality conceptual models.
- **Exclusion Criteria (EC)**:
  1. *EC1*: Non-peer-reviewed conference abstracts, editorial introductions, book reviews, and patent filings.
  2. *EC2*: Purely algorithmic computer science benchmarks lacking human behavioral interaction or consumer response variables.
  3. *EC3*: Duplicate records or publications with insufficient methodological disclosure.

### 2.3. Bibliometric Mapping Protocol (VOSviewer Configuration)
To analyze the intellectual structure without subjective researcher bias, the clean corpus of 1,441 studies was processed through **VOSviewer (version 1.6.21)**. A co-word occurrence analysis was deployed using fractional counting. A minimum occurrence threshold of $T = 5$ citations was enforced, resulting in 866 nodes interconnected by 250 structural co-word links. The association strength normalization method was utilized to compute coordinate clustering and network density.

#### Cluster 1 (Blue): Algorithmic Personalization Stimuli and Cognitive Fluency
Comprising 310 articles, this cluster investigates how recommendation engines serve as environmental stimuli ($S$). Core keywords include: *recommendation system, machine learning, perceived personalization, algorithm accuracy, serendipity, cognitive fit*. Research in this cluster demonstrates that modern consumers evaluate recommendation agents not merely as technical filters, but as social actors possessing competence and intent (Al-Natour et al., 2011; Komiak & Benbasat, 2006). When recommendations align tightly with personal preferences, cognitive processing fluency increases dramatically, reducing the search costs associated with evaluating product catalogs (Shanahan et al., 2019).

#### Cluster 2 (Red): Emotional Arousal, Impulsive Urges, and Behavioral Checkout
Encompassing 382 articles, this cluster focuses on consumer response mechanisms ($R$). Prominent keywords include: *impulse buying, urge to buy impulsively, spontaneous purchase, emotional arousal, hedonic shopping value, self-regulation failure*. Studies in this cluster highlight that impulse buying is primarily driven by affective intensity rather than rational utility maximization (Beatty & Ferrell, 1998; Rook & Fisher, 1995). Scholars emphasize that while the *Urge to Buy Impulsively (UBI)* is a psychological state of sudden desire, actual *Online Impulse Buying (OIB)* requires the breakdown or bypassing of cognitive self-control (Baumeister, 2002; Badgaiyan & Verma, 2015).

#### Cluster 3 (Green): E-Trust, Cumulative E-Satisfaction, and Experiential Organism
Containing 174 articles, this cluster acts as the theoretical connective tissue ($O$) binding algorithmic features to behavioral outcomes. Central keywords include: *e-satisfaction, customer satisfaction, trust, perceived risk, perceived usefulness, satisfaction disconfirmation*. This cluster demonstrates that transaction-specific satisfaction is insufficient to explain impulsive reactions. Rather, **cumulative e-satisfaction**, representing an aggregated psychological state of contentment derived from frictionless and continuous digital interactions, is vital. When algorithmic recommendations consistently deliver high accuracy and delightful novelty, consumers experience a state of affective comfort and platform trust that attenuates their perceived risk and analytical defense mechanisms (Xiao & Benbasat, 2007; Spreng et al., 1996; Verhagen & van Dolen, 2011).

## 4. INTEGRATIVE THEORETICAL FRAMEWORK AND HYPOTHESIS DEDUCTION

Drawing upon the synthesis of our 1,441-article corpus and the structural fault lines identified in bibliometric mapping, we formulate an integrative conceptual framework anchored in **Stimulus-Organism-Response (S-O-R)** theory of Mehrabian & Russell (1974), augmented by the **Cognitive Fit Theory** of Vessey (1991) and the **Strength Model of Self-Control** of Baumeister (2002).

### 4.1. Algorithmic Stimuli and Cumulative E-Satisfaction ($H_1, H_2, H_4$)
Perceived personalization refers to the degree to which a consumer perceives that a digital platform tailors its offerings to their unique preferences and shopping history (Shanahan et al., 2019; Srinivasan et al., 2002). According to Cognitive Fit Theory, when an information system provides visual and contextual cues that match the consumer's internal mental schema, cognitive effort is minimized, in line with Vessey (1991). High perceived personalization creates feelings of psychological intimacy and perceived platform intelligence (Al-Natour et al., 2011). When users perceive that recommendations are accurately calibrated to their tastes, their shopping efficiency rises, generating positive cognitive appraisal and cumulative satisfaction, corroborating Xiao & Benbasat (2007).

Simultaneously, algorithmic accuracy, the degree to which recommended items correctly reflect the user's specific functional requirements (Al-Natour et al., 2011), serves as a primary driver of expectancy confirmation. However, accuracy alone can result in redundant suggestions. Recommendation novelty and serendipity (the algorithmic presentation of unexpected yet attractive items; Zhang et al., 2021) introduce hedonic excitement into the browsing journey. When users encounter unexpected product discoveries, hedonic shopping value increases dramatically, culminating in enhanced cumulative e-satisfaction. Therefore, we propose:
- **$H_1$**: *Perceived personalization positively influences cumulative e-satisfaction.*
- **$H_2$**: *Perceived recommendation accuracy positively influences cumulative e-satisfaction.*
- **$H_4$**: *Recommendation novelty positively influences cumulative e-satisfaction.*

### 4.2. E-Satisfaction as the Bridge to Impulsive Urges ($H_3$)
In environmental psychology, internal organism states mediate the translation of ambient stimuli into behavioral urges. Traditional consumer decision models position impulse buying as an irrational departure from deliberate calculation, as conceptualized by Rook & Fisher (1995). However, through the lens of S-O-R, impulse buying is driven by immediate emotional arousal and positive affect (Beatty & Ferrell, 1998; Verhagen & van Dolen, 2011). 

Cumulative e-satisfaction produces a psychological state of affective comfort and disarmed vigilance. When a shopper experiences heightened satisfaction with the algorithmic interface, perceived transaction risk diminishes, and positive affect surges (Spreng et al., 1996; Oliver, 1993). This affective elevation disarms cognitive defense mechanisms, fostering a spontaneous openness to unbudgeted products and generating a powerful urge to buy impulsively ($UBI$). Thus, we propose:
- **$H_3$**: *Cumulative e-satisfaction positively influences the urge to buy impulsively.*
- **$H_{3a}$ (Mediation)**: *Cumulative e-satisfaction mediates the relationship between algorithmic stimuli (PP, ACC, NOV) and the urge to buy impulsively.*

### 4.3. The Urge-to-Behavior Transition ($H_5$)
The urge to buy impulsively represents a state of acute emotional desire that precedes action (Beatty & Ferrell, 1998; Rook & Fisher, 1995). In digital retail environments characterized by frictionless checkout architectures (e.g., Apple Pay, 1-click ordering, biometric authentication), the temporal latency between experiencing an impulse and executing payment is reduced to seconds. Prior empirical literature consistently validates that felt impulsive urges represent the most proximate psychological determinant of actual impulse buying behavior (Badgaiyan & Verma, 2015; Zheng et al., 2019). We therefore hypothesize:
- **$H_5$**: *The urge to buy impulsively positively influences online impulse buying behavior.*

### 4.4. Ego Depletion as the Psychological Boundary Condition ($H_6$)
A critical limitation of prior marketing literature is the assumption of a static, unvarying relationship between impulsive urges and checkout execution. According to Baumeister's (2002) Strength Model of Self-Control, human willpower operates as a finite physiological resource that depletes upon exertion (*Ego Depletion*; Baumeister et al., 2007; Tangney et al., 2004). 

Consumers possessing intact self-control resources possess the executive capacity to suppress spontaneous urges through deliberative cognitive override (resisting the temptation by contemplating long-term budgetary constraints). However, when consumers experience ego depletion, induced by extended daily cognitive exertion, multiple sequential decision trade-offs, or evening digital fatigue, their self-regulatory capacity becomes severely compromised. In this depleted psychological state, the executive control mechanisms necessary to inhibit the impulsive urge fail, enabling the affective urge ($UBI$) to convert directly into transactional purchasing ($OIB$). Consequently, we establish ego depletion as a key positive moderator:
- **$H_6$**: *Ego depletion positively moderates the relationship between the urge to buy impulsively and online impulse buying behavior, such that the relationship is significantly stronger when consumers experience high ego depletion.*

## 5. DISCUSSION, THEORETICAL CONTRIBUTIONS, AND MANAGERIAL IMPLICATIONS

### 5.1. Discussion of Synthesized Findings
Our systematic synthesis of 1,441 articles and bibliometric analysis of 866 seminal works resolves several enduring ambiguities in retailing literature. First, we demonstrate that algorithmic personalization does not trigger impulse buying through a simple, deterministic stimulus-response reflex. Rather, algorithmic shopping operates as a multi-stage cognitive-affective journey. Algorithms act as cognitive accelerators that deliver tailored value, which in turn elevates cumulative e-satisfaction. This satisfaction acts as the psychological "green light," lowering risk perceptions and allowing hedonic impulses to surface uninhibited.

Second, our framework reconciles the conflicting findings regarding personalization and consumer reactance. We demonstrate that recommendation novelty must be balanced with perceived accuracy. Overly aggressive personalization without novelty generates perceived algorithmic surveillance and uncanny valley discomfort (Blut et al., 2021), attenuating satisfaction. Conversely, calibrated recommendations that deliver serendipitous novelty within a trusted accuracy boundary yield peak e-satisfaction.

### 5.2. Theoretical Contributions
This study advances marketing and retailing scholarship in three substantial ways:
1. **Advancement of S-O-R in Algorithmic Environments**: We transition the S-O-R paradigm from static physical or web atmospherics to dynamic, agentic algorithmic commerce, formalizing algorithmic cues as active socio-technical stimuli.
2. **Re-conceptualization of E-Satisfaction as an In-Process Catalyst**: We bridge Oliver's expectancy-disconfirmation theory and environmental psychology by proving that e-satisfaction is not merely an outcome metric, but an active affective catalyst that disarms cognitive resistance during browsing.
3. **Integration of Ego Depletion into Marketing Models**: We bridge consumer psychology and quantitative behavioral modeling by demonstrating that self-regulation strength is the decisive boundary condition explaining why some impulsive urges convert while others dissipate.

### 5.3. Actionable Managerial Implications for Digital Retailers
- **Engineering Serendipitous Personalization (The 70/30 Rule)**: Retail algorithm designers must resist maximizing purely redundant collaborative filtering. Implementing a hybrid algorithmic strategy that balances high-accuracy items (70%) with serendipitous exploration items (30%) maximizes consumer discovery delight while sustaining e-satisfaction.
- **Timing and Ethical Nudging**: E-commerce platforms should recognize the temporal rhythms of consumer ego depletion (e.g., late-night browsing sessions). While conversion rates are highest during depleted states, aggressive exploitation risks post-purchase buyer remorse and elevated return rates. Retailers should deploy ethical algorithmic guardrails, including clear order confirmation windows and transparent return policies, to safeguard long-term brand loyalty.

### 5.4. Agenda for Future Empirical Research
To catalyze subsequent empirical research, we propose a 5-pillar research agenda:
1. **Methodological Triangulation (fsQCA & NCA)**: Future scholars should augment symmetric regression/PLS-SEM models with asymmetric configuration approaches (Fuzzy-Set Qualitative Comparative Analysis and Necessary Condition Analysis) to identify causal combinations of algorithmic attributes that produce extreme impulse purchases.
2. **Experimental Ego Depletion Manipulations**: Researchers should employ dual-task laboratory experiments (e.g., Stroop color-naming tests or cognitive depletion tasks) to causally test the moderating threshold of self-control failure.
3. **Eye-Tracking and Neuro-Marketing Telemetry**: Integrate physiological telemetry (pupillometry, galvanic skin response) to capture non-conscious emotional arousal during real-time recommendation encounters.
